OBJECTIVE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Experiment B as planned in docs/260602.iteration-2-plan.txt Part 3
proposed multiplying the per-position CFM squared-error contribution by
an inverse-frequency weight indexed by the target tile type. The
intent was to address two Iteration 1 failure modes simultaneously:
the dominant DiscontinuousGround pattern (62% of all Iteration 1
violations) and the rare-tile failure modes (BulletBill-related
fragments, isolated PipeTop pieces, FloatingEnemy positions).

This report documents what actually happened when the change was
implemented and run. Spoiler: the change produced one substantial
improvement (continuous ground rows in every chunk) but introduced a
new dominant failure mode (Breakable-brick over-prediction that
saturates most of every chunk). Per-chunk evidence follows.

  TileTypeFrequencyComputer.BuildInverseFrequencyWeightTensor(counts, device)
      Returns a 1D tensor on the training device where weight[i] =
      total_tiles / (counts[i] * 13). The 13 in the denominator is the
      count of nonzero tile types and normalizes the dataset-weighted
      mean of the weights to exactly 1.0. The actual weights produced
      from the training data:

        Solid                  0.911
        Breakable              3.227
        Enemy                 10.451
        PipeBodyLeft          15.289
        PipeBodyRight         15.299
        Coin                  16.865
        PipeTopLeft           32.869
        PipeTopRight          32.883
        QuestionEmpty         42.465
        QuestionFull          98.864
        BulletBillLauncher   180.350
        BulletBillBody       221.363

One new loss method was added to CfmLossComputer:

  ComputeWeightedLoss(model, x1, classWeights)
      Same as ComputeLoss except the per-element squared error
      tensor is multiplied by a per-position weight tensor extracted
      from the one-hot target tensor via channel-wise sum
      (x1 * classWeights.reshape(1, NumTileTypes, 1, 1)).sum(dim=1),
      and the result is reduced by mean.

CfmTrainingLoop was modified to compute the weight tensor once after
chunk loading and to call ComputeWeightedLoss inside the per-step
training loop instead of ComputeLoss.

The training entrypoint was changed to write checkpoints to
unet-class-balanced-checkpoint.bin and loss logs to
loss-log-class-balanced.csv so the ablation does not overwrite the
Iteration 1 baseline at unet-baseline-checkpoint.bin and loss-log.csv.

Training ran on the same dataset, same architecture, same optimizer,
same seed, same step count as the Iteration 1 20k baseline. The only
difference between the two runs is the per-position weighting of the
squared error.

PART 3: AGGREGATE FAILURE PATTERNS
================================================================================

Across all 10 chunks the following patterns hold consistently:

3.1 Breakable saturation
    Every chunk has Breakable tile occupancy between 65 percent and
    75 percent of the visible area. In the training data Breakable
    accounts for only 2.38 percent of all tile positions. The model
    is therefore over-predicting Breakable by a factor of roughly
    27 to 32 relative to the empirical training distribution.

3.2 Empty (sky) under-prediction
    Empty occupies only a 1-to-3-row band at the top of each chunk
    plus narrow horizontal strips between brick bands. Estimated
    Empty fraction per chunk: 12 to 20 percent. Training data Empty
    fraction: 86 percent. The model is under-predicting Empty by a
    factor of roughly 4 to 7.

3.3 Solid ground continuity (positive finding)
    Every chunk has a continuous Solid tile row at the bottom with
    visually zero gaps. This is the single dominant failure mode of
    Iteration 1 (DiscontinuousGround, 62 percent of all Iteration 1
    violations) and it is essentially solved by this Experiment B
    variant.

3.4 BulletBill misplacement
    All 10 chunks contain at least 1 BulletBillLauncher tile, and
    most contain 2 or 3. Every observed BulletBillLauncher tile is
    positioned in the top sky band of the chunk rather than at the
    bottom on top of a brick platform, where Mario 5-1 and Mario 7-1
    place them in the training data. None of the observed
    BulletBillLauncher tiles in the 10 chunks has a BulletBillBody
    tile in the cell directly below it; all are isolated. The model
    has learned that BulletBill tiles must appear (because of the
    high weight) but has not learned where they should appear or
    what they should connect to.

3.5 FloatingEnemy persists
    Most Enemy tiles in the 10 chunks are embedded inside the
    Breakable brick mass rather than standing on top of a Solid tile.
    This is the same FloatingEnemy failure mode observed in
    Iteration 1, made worse by the new pattern of Enemies being
    placed inside brick regions where Mario can never reach them.

3.6 Pipe structure occasionally valid
    Approximately 5 of the 10 chunks contain at least one
    structurally-valid 2-wide pipe (PipeTopLeft + PipeTopRight on
    one row, PipeBodyLeft + PipeBodyRight on the row below). The
    remaining pipe pieces are isolated fragments. This is a slight
    improvement over Iteration 1 where complete pipes were rare.

3.7 QuestionFull over-prediction
    Each chunk contains 5 to 12 QuestionFull tiles. Training data
    QuestionFull fraction is 0.08 percent (768 of 987056). The
    weight for QuestionFull was 98.864. The empirical generation
    rate for QuestionFull in the chunks is roughly 1.5 to 3 percent,
    so over-prediction by a factor of ~20 to 40.

3.8 Coin clustering into horizontal lines (correct pattern)
    Every chunk has at least one horizontal line of 4 to 9 adjacent
    coin tiles. This is the canonical Mario coin layout, so the
    model has learned a real spatial pattern here. However the model
    ALSO embeds coins inside brick masses, which is not a valid
    NES Mario configuration. So the pattern is partially learned.

PART 4: COMPARISON WITH ITERATION 1 BASELINE
================================================================================

The Iteration 1 20k-step unweighted baseline produced chunks with
mean total violations of 8.2 per chunk and a violation distribution
dominated by DiscontinuousGround (62 percent of all violations).
The visual character of those chunks was sparse: mostly Empty sky
with isolated brick fragments and a discontinuous ground row.

Iteration 2 Experiment B (raw inverse-frequency) produces chunks

    Property                 Iteration 1    Iteration 2 Exp B v1
    ------------------------ -------------- ----------------------
    Ground continuity        Poor           Excellent
    Sky preservation         Good           Poor (compressed)

    Pipe structure           Mostly broken  Sometimes valid

Neither variant produces chunks that pass a "is this a real Mario
level" visual inspection. They fail in different ways: Iteration 1
fails by under-generation (most positions stay close to Empty);
Iteration 2 Experiment B v1 fails by over-generation (most positions
become Breakable or another upweighted type).

PART 5: ROOT CAUSE HYPOTHESIS
================================================================================

The raw inverse-frequency weighting in
TileTypeFrequencyComputer.BuildInverseFrequencyWeightTensor produces
weights with a ratio of 2487 between the highest (BulletBillBody at
221.363) and the lowest nonzero (Empty at 0.089). This 2487:1 ratio
exists in a system where the loss is mean squared error over the
one-hot velocity prediction.

The hypothesis for the over-prediction effect:

  1. During training, the gradient at a position where the target
     is Empty contributes 0.089 to the loss. The gradient at a
     position where the target is Breakable contributes 3.227 to
     the loss, BulletBill contributes 221.363.

  2. The model has limited capacity (BaseChannels = 64, approximately
     3 million parameters). It cannot perfectly predict the spatial
     distribution of every tile type. When it makes prediction
     errors, the gradient pulls it toward whichever class would
     reduce the loss most.

  3. With Empty at weight 0.089, the cost of falsely predicting
     non-Empty at an Empty position is small. With Breakable at
     weight 3.227, the cost of falsely predicting Breakable at a
     Breakable position is moderate but the benefit of correctly
     predicting Breakable is also moderate. With BulletBill at 221,
     correctly predicting it is highly rewarded but falsely
     predicting non-BulletBill at a BulletBill position is severely
     penalized.

  4. The Nash equilibrium that the optimizer finds is: predict
     non-Empty tiles much more often than warranted by the training
     distribution. The cheapest way to avoid the severe penalty for
     missing a BulletBill or QuestionFull is to occasionally predict
     these rare types, even in positions where they do not belong.
     The cheapest way to avoid penalties on Breakable positions is
     to predict Breakable broadly. The penalty for predicting any of
     these in an actually-Empty position is small (0.089 weight on
     Empty positions), so the model is not punished much for spam.

  5. The result: the model becomes a non-Empty-tile spammer. The
     specific tile it spams most (Breakable) is the one that has a
     moderate weight (3.227) and moderate training frequency, so
     it is the "cheapest spam." Less spam of BulletBill and
     QuestionFull because their weight is so high that spamming
     them is risky (high penalty for false negatives in any of the
     few real BulletBill/QuestionFull positions).

This is a well-documented failure mode of raw class-balanced loss in
the segmentation literature. See Eigen and Fergus 2015 (their
median-frequency balancing), Cui et al. 2019 (effective number of
samples), and Lin et al. 2017 (focal loss) for canonical
remediations.


================================================================================
PART 6: PROPOSED REMEDIATION
================================================================================

Three remediations are candidate, in order of effort:

6.1 Square-root inverse-frequency weighting (smallest change)

    Replace the linear inverse-frequency formula with its square
    root. This compresses both the upweighting of rare types and
    the downweighting of common types, capping the dynamic range of
    the weights at approximately 50:1 instead of 2487:1.

    Expected weights after sqrt:
      Empty               0.298  (was 0.089)
      Solid               0.954  (was 0.911)
      Breakable           1.797  (was 3.227)
      Enemy               3.232  (was 10.451)
      Coin                4.108  (was 16.865)
      QuestionFull        9.943  (was 98.864)
      BulletBillBody     14.879  (was 221.363)
      BulletBillLauncher 13.431  (was 180.350)

    Effort: one new method in TileTypeFrequencyComputer (already
    drafted in the iteration-2-class-balanced-loss branch), one
    line change in CfmTrainingLoop. Build, train 20k steps,
    regenerate, evaluate.

6.2 Effective Number of Samples weighting (more principled)

    Implement the Cui et al. 2019 effective-number weighting where
    weight[i] = (1 - beta) / (1 - beta^count[i]) with beta close to
    1 (typical 0.9999). This gives diminishing-returns weight to
    additional samples of a class and produces a more conservative
    weight distribution than sqrt-inverse.

    Effort: one new method in TileTypeFrequencyComputer, one line
    change in CfmTrainingLoop. Slightly more arithmetic but no
    architectural change.

6.3 Focal loss (replace MSE)

    Replace the per-pixel squared error with a focal loss that
    down-weights easy examples (positions the model already
    predicts correctly) and up-weights hard ones. This is the
    Lin et al. 2017 approach and is generally considered the
    state-of-the-art for class-imbalanced classification.
    Implementation is more involved because the current CFM
    objective is MSE-on-velocity rather than cross-entropy-on-
    class-probabilities, so the focal modification has to be
    adapted to the velocity prediction.

    Effort: substantial. Multiple sessions of focused work.

Recommended next step: Experiment B v2 = sqrt-inverse-frequency
(Option 6.1). The code is mostly written already on the current
branch; finalize, build, train, regenerate, compare against this
result. If the sqrt variant produces something between the
Iteration 1 sparse failure and the Experiment B v1 dense failure,
the experiment is on a productive track and can be iterated
further. If not, escalate to Option 6.2.
